The Śrī Yantra, Explained

A companion reference for the copper-plate scan in this project. Each section maps onto the region groups in the viewer — when you trace a mantra in edit mode, the name lists below are what you will be matching against the engravings.

A note on reliability. Everything here is assembled from the traditional Śrī Vidyā corpus (texts listed in §8). The broad structure — nine āvaraṇas, their names, the deity lists — is stable across sources. Details vary between lineages: the exact order of names within a ring, whether eight or ten siddhis are counted, minor spellings. Where the plate disagrees with a "standard" list, trust the plate for transcription and note the variant.

1 · What it is

The Śrī Yantra (श्रीयन्त्र, śrīyantra), also called the Śrī Cakra (श्रीचक्र), is the central sacred diagram of the Śrī Vidyā tradition — a South-Asian tantric lineage devoted to the goddess Lalitā Tripurasundarī ("the beautiful one of the three worlds/cities"). It is often called the yantrarāja, king of yantras: in Śrī Vidyā the diagram is not an illustration of the goddess but her actual body in geometric form. Worship offered to the yantra is worship offered to her.

A yantra (from the root yam, "to restrain, support") is an instrument — a support for meditation and ritual. Copper plates like this one are engraved, ritually consecrated (prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā, "installing the life-breath"), and then treated as a living seat of the deity: bathed, anointed, offered flowers, and used as the ground for the navāvaraṇa pūjā described in §7.

Three ideas organize everything else:

  1. Emanation and return. The yantra is a map of the cosmos unfolding from a single point (the bindu) outward into multiplicity, and equally a map of the practitioner's return journey inward. Reading it outward-in is dissolution (saṁhāra); inward-out is creation (sṛṣṭi).
  2. Śiva and Śakti. The interlocking triangles encode the union of consciousness (Śiva, upward triangles) and energy (Śakti, downward triangles). Neither exists apart; the diagram is their intercourse.
  3. Macrocosm = microcosm. The Bhāvanopaniṣad maps every ring of the yantra onto the human body and psyche — the sixteen "attractor" goddesses are your own sixteen powers of perception and appetite, and so on. The yantra is simultaneously the universe and you.

2 · The geometry

At the center is the bindu (बिन्दु, "point"). Around it, nine interlocking triangles — four pointing up (Śiva, called śrīkaṇṭha triangles) and five pointing down (Śakti, śivayuvati triangles). Their intersections generate 43 small triangles, arranged in concentric rings:

Around the triangles: an eight-petaled lotus (aṣṭadala), a sixteen-petaled lotus (ṣoḍaśadala), three circles (vṛtta-traya or mekhalā-traya, the three girdles), and finally the bhūpura (भूपुर, "earth-city") — the square rampart of three nested lines with four T-shaped gates opening to the cardinal directions.

A traditional verse — engraved twice on this very plate (upper-left and lower-right text blocks) — enumerates the structure:

बिन्दुत्रिकोणवसुकोणदशारयुग्म-मन्वस्रनागदलसंयुतषोडशारम् ।
वृत्तत्रयं च धरणीसदनत्रयं च श्रीचक्रमेतदुदितं परदेवतायाः ॥ bindu-trikoṇa-vasukoṇa-daśāra-yugma- manvasra-nāgadala-saṁyuta-ṣoḍaśāram | vṛtta-trayaṁ ca dharaṇī-sadana-trayaṁ ca śrīcakram etad uditaṁ paradevatāyāḥ || "Point, triangle, the eight-cornered, the pair of ten-spoked, the fourteen-spoked, together with the eight petals and the sixteen — the three circles and the three earth-abodes: this is declared the Śrī Cakra of the Supreme Goddess."

The number-words are the standard Sanskrit code: vasu = 8, manu = 14, nāga = 8. The plate's engraving is a slight variant of this verse — worth transcribing exactly as cut.

The Saundaryalaharī (v. 11) gives the same census poetically: four Śiva triangles, five Śakti triangles, and with the lotuses, circles and lines the goddess's dwelling resolves into forty-four corners (43 triangles + bindu).

Flat vs. raised. A yantra drawn or engraved flat, like this plate, is a bhū-prastāra. The same geometry projected into a stepped pyramid is a meru-prastāra or Mahāmeru, identifying the yantra with Mount Meru, the world-axis. (The small stepped-pyramid figure engraved on the upper right of this plate is almost certainly a Meru glyph — a reminder of the 3-D reading.)

Construction note. Drawing a correct Śrī Cakra is genuinely hard — the nine triangles must close on shared intersection points, and manuscripts transmit competing constructions (concurrent vs. non-concurrent solutions). Slight asymmetries in a hand-engraved plate are normal and not defects.

3 · The nine āvaraṇas — overview

The yantra is worshipped as nine concentric āvaraṇas (आवरण, "enclosures", "veils"). Each has a name describing its power, a resident class of yoginīs, a presiding goddess (cakreśvarī), an associated mudrā, and a fruit (siddhi). Eight goddesses of Anaṅga — "the bodiless one," i.e. Kāma, god of love. They govern the stirring (kṣobha) that sets creation in motion. Several are legible on the plate's inner petals:

Āvaraṇa 7 — the eight triangles (Sarva-roga-hara cakra)

The eight Vāg-devatās, goddesses of speech — in tradition, the very authors of the Lalitā Sahasranāma. "Removing all disease" here includes the root disease, ignorance:

वशिनी Vaśinī· कामेश्वरी Kāmeśvarī· मोदिनी Modinī· विमला Vimalā· अरुणा Aruṇā· जयिनी Jayinī· सर्वेश्वरी Sarveśvarī· कौलिनी Kaulinī

Āvaraṇa 8 — the central triangle (Sarva-siddhi-prada cakra)

The innermost downward triangle is the goddess's immediate presence. Its three corners hold three great śaktis:

कामेश्वरी Kāmeśvarī (corner of agni, fire)· वज्रेश्वरी Vajreśvarī (corner of sūrya, sun)· भगमालिनी Bhagamālinī (corner of soma, moon)

The three sides are also read as the three pīṭhas (seats) of the goddess — Kāmarūpa, Pūrṇagiri, Jālandhara — and the triangle as her kāmakalā, the creative desire of consciousness. Around this zone the plate engraves Lalitā's four weapons as śaktis: धनुःशक्तिः (the sugarcane bow), बाणशक्तिः (the five flower arrows), पाशशक्तिः (the noose), and अङ्कुशशक्तिः (the goad) — the bow and noose labels are clearly legible just above the bindu on this plate.

Āvaraṇa 9 — the bindu (Sarvānanda-maya cakra)

The point at the center is Mahātripurasundarī herself, seated in union with Kāmeśvara (Śiva as lord of desire). It is the sarvānandamaya cakra, "formed of all bliss" — the undivided source that the eight rings progressively veil, and the destination of the inward journey. On this plate the bindu is engraved as a small circle flanked by the bīja syllables क्लीं (klīṁ) and सौः (sauḥ).

5 · The goddess and her court

Lalitā Tripurasundarī (ललिता त्रिपुरसुन्दरी) — "the playful one, beautiful in the three cities" — is the supreme deity of Śrī Vidyā, worshipped as the highest form of the Great Goddess. "Three cities" is read many ways: the three worlds, the three states (waking, dream, deep sleep), the three guṇas, the three bodies. She holds four emblems: sugarcane bow (the mind), five flower arrows (the five senses), noose (attachment), goad (aversion/impulsion). The dhyāna verse engraved across this plate's two bottom corners (§6) describes her for visualization.

The fifteen (sixteen) Nityās. The lunar days (tithis) of the waxing fortnight are fifteen goddesses, the nityās ("eternals"), with Lalitā herself as the sixteenth (ṣoḍaśī) — the full moon beyond change. They are often engraved around the central rings of a Śrī Yantra, and a numbered list on the left side of this plate appears to enumerate deities in this family:

कामेश्वरी Kāmeśvarī· भगमालिनी Bhagamālinī· नित्यक्लिन्ना Nityaklinnā· भेरुण्डा Bheruṇḍā· वह्निवासिनी Vahnivāsinī· महावज्रेश्वरी Mahāvajreśvarī· शिवदूती Śivadūtī· त्वरिता Tvaritā· कुलसुन्दरी Kulasundarī· नित्या Nityā· नीलपताका Nīlapatākā· विजया Vijayā· सर्वमङ्गला Sarvamaṅgalā· ज्वालामालिनी Jvālāmālinī· चित्रा Citrā

The root mantra of the tradition, so central it is simply called the Vidyā. Fifteen syllables in three groups (kūṭas):

Adding the secret sixteenth syllable श्रीं (śrīṁ) makes it the Ṣoḍaśī mantra, reserved for initiates. The syllables are held to be the sound-body of the goddess exactly as the yantra is her form-body — mantra, yantra, and devī are one thing in three registers. Fragments of these kūṭa syllables (and related bījas) are engraved in and around the central triangle of this plate.

Bālā — the young-goddess mantra

ऐं क्लीं सौः (aiṁ klīṁ sauḥ) — the three-bīja mantra of Bālā Tripurasundarī, the goddess as a nine-year-old girl; usually the first mantra given to a Śrī Vidyā student. All three syllables appear around this plate's center; ऐं ह्रीं श्रीं together are engraved below the yantra near the bottom gate.

7 · How it is worshipped

The classical rite is the Navāvaraṇa Pūjā (श्रीचक्रपूजा): the worshipper honors each enclosure in sequence, reciting the name of every yoginī with offerings, showing the āvaraṇa's mudrā, honoring its cakreśvarī, and only then stepping inward — nine thresholds between the everyday world and the bindu. The Khaḍgamālā Stotra ("garland of the sword") is effectively this same journey compressed into a single recitation: it strings together the names of every deity of every ring in order. If you want one text that "reads" the yantra aloud, region by region, it is the Khaḍgamālā — an ideal cross-check while annotating.

Three ritual "orders" (krama) govern direction of travel:

  • sṛṣṭi-krama (creation): center → outward, the cosmos unfolding;
  • sthiti-krama (maintenance): honoring the balance;
  • saṁhāra-krama (dissolution): rim → center, the practitioner's path — the most common order for the pūjā, ending at the bindu.

The Bhāvanopaniṣad teaches a fully internalized worship: each āvaraṇa is contemplated as a layer of oneself (the nine enclosures as nine sheaths of the body-mind; the fifteen nityās as the fifteen days of the moon and breaths of the day), so the entire rite can be performed in awareness alone.

Practical conventions for a plate like this: installed facing east (or the worshipper facing east), consecrated by prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā, kept on the altar, never treated as decor once consecrated; offerings of kumkum (vermilion) are typical — many old copper yantras carry red residue in the grooves.

8 · Where it comes from

Śrī Vidyā crystallized as a textual tradition roughly in the 8th–12th centuries CE, with roots older. Its anchor scriptures:

  • Vāmakeśvara Tantra — comprising the Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava ("ocean of the sixteen nityās") and the Yoginīhṛdaya ("heart of the yoginī"), the single most important source for the meaning of the cakra's parts.
  • Tantrarāja Tantra — encyclopedic ritual manual.
  • Lalitā Sahasranāma and Lalitā Triśatī (from the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa) — the thousand and three hundred names; the Sahasranāma's frame-story has the Vāg-devatās of āvaraṇa 7 as its composers.
  • Saundaryalaharī ("wave of beauty," attributed to Ādi Śaṅkara) — hundred verses on the goddess; v. 11 describes the yantra.
  • Bhāvanopaniṣad — the internalization manual.
  • Khaḍgamālā Stotra — the deity-roll of the nine āvaraṇas.

The tradition remains fully alive: the Śaṅkara maṭhas (Śṛṅgeri, Kāñcī) maintain Śrī Cakra worship — Kāmākṣī of Kāñcīpuram is worshipped primarily as the Śrī Cakra installed before her image — and household navāvaraṇa pūjā continues throughout South India and Nepal. Devanagari-lettered plates like this one are typical of North Indian / Nepalese workshops (South Indian plates more often use Telugu, Kannada or Grantha letters).

10 · Annotation cross-checks

While tracing regions in the viewer, keep these in mind:

  1. Count before you read. If a ring should have 14 names and you find 13, a name is hiding in damage or a ligature — the lists above tell you what to look for.
  2. Repeated names are real. Saṁkṣobhiṇī / Vidrāviṇī / Ākarṣiṇī appear in both the mudrā list (bhūpura) and the 14-triangle ring.
  3. Numbers on the plate are your friends. The engraver numbered many names (१, २, ३…) — the numeral tells you which ring and position, even when the name itself is hard to read.
  4. Trust the plate over the list for spelling; note variants in the region's explanation field.
  5. The vowels on the 16 petals and the bījas near the bindu are the likeliest places for worn or stylized letterforms — the letter reference has the tables for decoding them.
